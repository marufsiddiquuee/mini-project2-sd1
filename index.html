Beyond our solar system

A galaxy is a city of stars, held together by gravity

Every galaxy is a huge collection of stars, gas, dust and dark matter. Our own, the Milky Way, contains roughly 100 to 400 billion stars. This guide walks through how galaxies are built, how astronomers classify them, and how big they really are.

Start exploring

Anatomy of a galaxy

Most large galaxies share the same basic parts, though their sizes and proportions differ.

Nucleus

The bright centre. Most large galaxies hold a supermassive black hole here, often millions of times the mass of the Sun.

Bulge

A dense ball of mostly older stars surrounding the nucleus.

Disc

A flat, rotating plane of gas, dust and younger stars. This is where spiral arms appear.

Halo

A faint, spherical region of old star clusters, surrounded by a much larger halo of dark matter.

Types of galaxies

Astronomer Edwin Hubble sorted galaxies by shape in the 1920s. Scientists still use a version of his system today.

Spiral

Flat discs with curved arms of young, bright blue stars and star-forming gas. Spirals make up a large share of bright nearby galaxies, though the exact share depends on the survey.

Examples: Milky Way, Whirlpool Galaxy (M51)

Elliptical

Smooth, round or oval shapes with mostly older, reddish stars and very little gas. They range from nearly round to highly stretched.

Examples: Messier 87 (M87), M49

Irregular

No clear shape. Often disturbed by collisions or gravity from neighbouring galaxies, and frequently full of active star formation.

Examples: Large Magellanic Cloud, Small Magellanic Cloud

Did you know? About two thirds of spiral galaxies have a central bar of stars across the nucleus. The Milky Way is thought to be one of them.

Key terms

Light-year
The distance light travels in one year, about 9.46 trillion kilometres.
Dark matter
An invisible substance that does not emit light but whose gravity holds galaxies together.
Star-forming region
A patch of dense gas and dust where new stars are being born.
Local Group
The cluster of more than 50 galaxies that includes the Milky Way and Andromeda.

More facts

How many galaxies are there?

Estimates vary with the method used. Older counts suggested around 100 billion galaxies, while later estimates based on deep images push the number much higher, possibly around two trillion.

Will the Milky Way collide with Andromeda?

Yes, in about 4.5 billion years, although the stars themselves are so far apart that most will not hit each other. The two galaxies are expected to merge into one larger elliptical galaxy.

What is at the Milky Way’s centre?

A supermassive black hole called Sagittarius A*, with about four million times the mass of the Sun. It is about 26,000 light-years from Earth.
